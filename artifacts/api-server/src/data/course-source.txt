BASIC TENETS OF PSYCHOANALYSIS
An Oral Course

FORMAT
This is an oral class. The AI lecturer delivers each lecture by voice. After each lecture, the AI asks that lecture's six questions aloud, and the student answers orally in one or two sentences. Each answer is scored on the spot. After Lecture 5, the AI conducts the final oral exam.

========================================
LECTURE 1: THE HIDDEN MIND
Topics: the unconscious; psychic determinism; repression; drives; the pleasure and reality principles; id, ego, superego
========================================

Introduction. Psychoanalysis starts from one claim: you know far less about your own mind than you think. The reasons you give for your actions are a small, filtered part of what is actually happening in you. Today's six ideas form a single argument. Behavior outruns conscious reasons, so there is an unconscious. Nothing mental is random, so the unconscious can be traced. Some contents are actively hidden, which is repression. What gets hidden are wishes, which come from drives. Drives meet reality under two principles. The mind manages all of this through three agencies.

1. The unconscious. Common sense says the mind is transparent to itself: if I want something, I know it. This is false. A hypnotized subject is told he will open an umbrella indoors when the clock strikes. He wakes with no memory of the instruction, opens the umbrella on cue, and when asked why, invents a reason, such as checking it for holes. The real cause was a mental state he could not access. The same thing happens without hypnosis. A man swears he is over his ex, yet takes the route past her apartment every day. Some unconscious content is merely unattended. The important kind is dynamically unconscious: it is kept from awareness because knowing it would hurt. We infer it the way we infer anyone's mind, from behavior that only that state would explain.

2. Psychic determinism. No physical event is uncaused, and psychoanalysis applies the same rule to the mind: no mental event is accidental. Slips, forgettings, and "random" choices all have causes, usually motives. Ask someone for a random number and have them associate to it, and it routinely turns out to be an address, an age, or a parent's death date. As a result, everything counts as evidence, and nothing a person says or does can be dismissed as noise. Most mental events are overdetermined: several causes converge on them, so a single slip can carry more than one meaning.

3. Repression. Why are certain things unconscious? Not through ordinary forgetting. Repression is motivated exclusion. The mind pushes threatening content out of awareness and spends continuous effort keeping it out. The signs are selectivity and resistance. A woman remembers every childhood summer except the one when a family friend lived in the house, and she grows uneasy when questioned about it. Because keeping something repressed takes effort, the repressed returns whenever that effort slackens: in dreams, fatigue, intoxication, and symptoms. Distinguish this from suppression, which is the conscious decision not to think about something. Repression happens without the person knowing they are doing it.

4. Drives. What gets repressed is wishes, and wishes come from drives. A drive has a bodily source, a pressure, an aim (satisfaction), and an object (whatever happens to satisfy it). The object is the most variable part, which is why drives can be redirected, disguised, and displaced. The two great drives are sexual, understood broadly as the pursuit of pleasure and attachment, and aggressive. Drives are the engine of mental life. Without them there would be nothing to repress and no conflict. Their force shows when they are blocked: restless irritability that disappears the moment a suppressed desire is acknowledged.

5. The pleasure principle and the reality principle. The infant mind runs on the pleasure principle. It seeks immediate discharge of tension and, when frustrated, even hallucinates satisfaction. Hallucination does not feed anyone, so reality forces a second principle. The reality principle introduces delay, planning, and thought, which is essentially trial action carried out in the head. A toddler grabs the cookie; a ten-year-old waits until after dinner and gets two. The reality principle does not abolish the pleasure principle. It serves it more effectively. The pleasure principle keeps a protected reserve in daydreams and fantasy, where reality never has to be consulted.

6. Id, ego, superego. The mind is organized into three agencies. The id is the reservoir of drives. It is illogical, timeless, and tolerant of contradiction, and it demands satisfaction now. The ego perceives reality, tests it, delays action, and builds defenses, and much of its work is itself unconscious. The superego is internalized parental authority: the conscience that forbids and the ideal that demands, often harsher than the actual parents were. In a single morning you can see all three. You want to skip work (id), you feel guilty at the thought (superego), and you call in for a half day (ego). The ego serves three masters: the id, the superego, and reality. Mental health is the ego managing that negotiation. Neurosis is the ego failing at it.

Conclusion. Put the pieces together and you have the psychoanalytic picture of the mind. It is driven by wishes, split against itself, and actively hiding parts of itself from itself. Yet every part can be traced, because nothing in it is accidental. Lecture 2 maps where hidden contents sit, how the drives develop through childhood, and how these conflicts produce defenses and symptoms.

LECTURE 1: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. A researcher sincerely praises a collaborator in conversation but repeatedly leaves that person's name off shared award nominations. She becomes defensive when the omission is mentioned. Is simple absentmindedness an adequate explanation, or is an unconscious conflict a stronger possibility? Justify your judgment and say what further evidence could change it. (Answer: Weigh the repeated selective omissions and defensiveness as possible evidence of conflicted wishes about recognition against workload or administrative explanations. A supported interpretation connects the pattern to a motive outside awareness without treating the motive as established fact and identifies discriminating evidence such as how she handles other collaborators.)
2. A student accidentally sends an unfinished complaint to a lecturer just after insisting he has no grievance. He also reports having slept only three hours. One observer calls the mistake meaningless; another says it proves hostility. Adjudicate between these claims using psychic determinism. (Answer: A caused event need not have one proven unconscious meaning. Consider both the grievance and fatigue as potentially converging causes, distinguish causal explanation from certainty about motive, and judge the absolute claims too strong unless additional context supports them. Reasoned alternatives grounded in the details are acceptable.)
3. A lawyer can describe an old workplace in detail but repeatedly draws a blank around one humiliating dismissal and becomes tense when discussing it. A colleague says this must be repression. Is that conclusion justified? Explain how you would distinguish repression, ordinary forgetting, and a conscious wish not to discuss the event. (Answer: Selective difficulty and tension make motivated exclusion a plausible hypothesis but do not prove it. Distinguish an involuntary barrier with resistance from ordinary incomplete memory and from knowingly withholding a remembered event. Seek non-leading contextual evidence and do not invent forgotten events.)
4. After being passed over for promotion, a chef begins intense competitive training. His irritability subsides, although the promotion decision has not changed. Has the underlying pressure disappeared, or might its route to satisfaction have changed? Defend an interpretation and identify something that would make you reconsider it. (Answer: Consider aggressive pressure finding a different aim or object through competition rather than assuming that the workplace wish vanished. Link the change in activity and tension to the interpretation, while allowing exercise, companionship, or other explanations. Distinguish evidence for redirection from a mere label.)
5. A musician declines an enjoyable concert to save for an instrument she wants, but still looks forward to performing with it. Her friend says she has stopped pursuing pleasure. Is the friend right? Explain how your judgment would change if she kept denying herself even after she could comfortably afford the instrument. (Answer: Delaying immediate enjoyment for a more durable satisfaction illustrates reality-guided pursuit of pleasure rather than its abandonment. Continued deprivation after the practical reason disappears raises a different question about prohibition or conflict. Apply the distinction to the changed facts rather than reciting the two principles.)
6. A scientist wants to leave a tedious experiment to attend a celebration, feels morally worthless for considering it, and arranges reliable cover so she can attend the last hour. Is the arrangement necessarily a failure of self-control, or can it be a workable compromise? Explain the competing demands and what would make the compromise unhealthy. (Answer: Identify the wish for enjoyment, the harsh internal demand, and the real obligation, then explain how a negotiated solution can serve all three. Assess whether the arrangement actually meets the obligation and whether punishment or persistent distress makes it unstable. Naming id, ego, and superego without applying them is insufficient.)

LECTURE 2: MAPS, STAGES, AND DEFENSES
Topics: the topographic model; infantile sexuality; the Oedipus complex; fixation and regression; defense mechanisms; symptom formation
========================================

Introduction. Lecture 1 showed that the mind hides wishes from itself. Today we ask three questions. Where do hidden contents sit? Where do the wishes come from developmentally? What does the mind do to keep them hidden? The answers lead to the central result of this lecture: a symptom is not a malfunction but a solution, and a bad one.

7. The topographic model. Before the structural model came a simpler map with three regions. The conscious is whatever you are aware of right now. The preconscious holds what you can retrieve on demand, like your phone number or yesterday's lunch. The unconscious holds what is barred from awareness and cannot be retrieved at will, such as a forbidden childhood wish. The important border is the censorship between the unconscious and the preconscious. The map's weakness is that the censor itself works unconsciously, which is why the structural model later supplemented it. Use the topographic model for where a content is. Use the structural model for who wants it and who opposes it.

8. Infantile sexuality and the stages. The most resisted claim in psychoanalysis is that sexuality begins in infancy. "Sexual" here means the pursuit of bodily pleasure in general, not adult genital sex. That pursuit centers on different zones in a fixed order. In the oral stage, pleasure comes from sucking and feeding. In the anal stage, it comes from retaining and expelling, and so from control; a toddler's pride and stubbornness over toilet training is the clearest example. In the phallic stage, pleasure focuses on the genitals and on curiosity about sexual difference. Latency follows, a quiet period in which these interests are set aside. Genital sexuality arrives at puberty and gathers the earlier pleasures into adult form. Each stage leaves a residue in character, and adult personality is in large part the history of these stages.

9. The Oedipus complex and conscience. In the phallic stage the child wants exclusive possession of one parent and treats the other as a rival. This produces wishes the child cannot act on and fears punishment for. The resolution is decisive: the child gives up the rivalry and identifies with the rival, becoming like the parent instead of replacing them. With that identification, the parent's prohibitions move inside. A boy stops competing with his father, starts imitating him, and now hears his father's rules as his own voice. This is how the superego forms. Conscience is not innate. It is an inheritance from a conflict, and that is why it can be cruel: it carries the force of the aggression that was turned away from the parent.

10. Fixation and regression. When a stage brings too much frustration or too much gratification, part of the libido stays attached there. That is a fixation. Fixations are weak points. Under later stress, the person regresses: they fall back to the satisfactions of the stage where they are fixated. After a layoff, a man starts chain-eating and chewing pens. That is oral regression, a return to the earliest source of comfort. The stress does not create the regression; it follows a path laid down in childhood. The fixation point also predicts the form of the regression. An anally fixated person under stress becomes rigid, hoarding, and controlling, not hungry.

11. Defense mechanisms. When a forbidden wish nears awareness, the ego defends itself, unconsciously. Denial refuses the fact itself: "I'm not an alcoholic." Projection puts your own impulse into someone else: I hate him becomes he hates me. Reaction formation adopts the opposite of the impulse, as in the crusader against homosexuality who has same-sex desires. The excess is the tell. Sublimation redirects the impulse toward a valued aim, as when aggression becomes surgery. It is the only defense that actually discharges the drive and doesn't just block it. Rationalization puts a respectable reason in place of the real one: "I didn't want that job anyway." Every defense trades truth for comfort.

12. Symptom formation. When defenses fail, symptoms form, and a symptom is a compromise. It expresses the forbidden wish and the defense against it at the same time. Take a hand paralysis with no physical cause, in a man who wanted to strike his hated father. The paralysis enacts the aggression, since the hand is now the center of his life, and blocks it, since the hand cannot strike. Both sides of the conflict get partial satisfaction, and that is why symptoms are so stubborn: removing one means reopening the conflict it settled. Treatment does not attack the symptom. It finds the conflict the symptom is resolving.

Conclusion. Childhood drives pass through stages and leave fixations. The Oedipal conflict installs a conscience. Forbidden wishes are held down by defenses, and when the defenses fail, the result is a symptom that half-satisfies everyone. Lecture 3 turns to the places where hidden wishes surface: dreams, slips, free association, and the relationship with the analyst.

LECTURE 2: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. A pianist can recall a melody while chatting with friends but goes blank whenever a formerly humiliating teacher asks her to play it. One explanation is that the melody is simply unavailable; another is that access is being inhibited. Which better fits the case, and how do the topographic and structural models help you distinguish them? (Answer: Successful recall elsewhere supports availability, while the selective context suggests inhibition that might be defensive or ordinary performance anxiety. Use the topographic distinction between retrievable and barred content and the structural account of conflict or censorship, without claiming that the pattern alone proves repression.)
2. A preschooler enjoys messy play but becomes especially absorbed in deciding when toys may be put away and who may touch his carefully arranged collection. An adult calls this evidence of adult-type sexual wishes. Assess that claim using the lecture's developmental account, and explain what the behavior does and does not warrant inferring. (Answer: Within the course's broad account of bodily pleasure and control, the child's enjoyment can be considered in developmental terms rather than equated with adult genital desire. Explain the role of retaining, ordering, or controlling while recognizing ordinary play and autonomy as alternatives. A single behavior does not establish fixation or pathology.)
3. A girl who resented her mother's closeness to her father begins admiring her mother and enforcing her mother's rules even when neither parent is present. Is this better understood as simple obedience or as a possible internal change? Defend your judgment and explain what evidence would distinguish identification from fear of being caught. (Answer: Consider movement from rivalry toward identification and internalization of prohibitions, connecting the unsupervised rule enforcement to possible superego formation. Contrast this with compliance dependent on surveillance or punishment. Treat the Oedipal reading as a supported possibility rather than the only possible developmental explanation.)
4. After a business failure, an executive begins obsessively accumulating and sorting inexpensive objects. His partner calls it a practical response to insecurity; an analyst suspects regression. What would you need to know to adjudicate between those readings, and why would stress alone not establish a fixation? (Answer: Explain how regression would mean a return to an earlier mode of satisfaction organized around retaining or control. Weigh the activity's practical usefulness, its emotional function, prior patterns, and developmental history. Stress may trigger many responses and cannot by itself identify a childhood fixation.)
5. A manager who recently mocked a junior colleague becomes convinced that the colleague is secretly trying to humiliate him. The colleague has also made one genuine complaint. Is projection a sufficient explanation, or might it be an oversimplification? Give a reasoned assessment and specify what evidence matters. (Answer: Relate possible projection to the manager's own hostile impulse being attributed to the colleague, but give weight to the actual complaint and other external evidence. Distinguish an amplified defensive reading from justified concern and allow a mixed explanation. Merely calling the manager defensive does not answer the case.)
6. A singer repeatedly loses her voice when asked to give a congratulatory speech for a sister she envies, yet can sing comfortably alone. Someone proposes that the problem both expresses and blocks hostility. Does that account explain the pattern, and what would make you reject or qualify it? (Answer: Explain the proposed compromise between an unacceptable hostile wish and a prohibition against expressing it, connecting both sides to this particular loss of voice. Consider performance anxiety and physical causes, and identify evidence that could weaken the interpretation. Do not diagnose a psychogenic condition from the vignette or assume medical causes have been excluded.)

LECTURE 3: WHERE THE HIDDEN SURFACES
Topics: dreams as wish fulfillment; dream work; parapraxes; free association; transference; countertransference
========================================

Introduction. The repressed does not stay quiet. It returns wherever the ego's guard drops: in sleep, in slips, and in the room with the analyst. Today we look at those openings and at the method built to exploit them.

13. Dreams as wish fulfillment. Every dream is the disguised fulfillment of a wish. What you remember, the plot and the images, is the manifest content. What it means, the wish it fulfills, is the latent content. The disguise exists because the censor still works during sleep, only at reduced strength. The wish gets through, but only in costume. A dream about attending a stranger's funeral can carry a wish for a rival's death. Anxiety dreams are not counterexamples. They are cases where the disguise failed and the wish came too close, so the censor sounded the alarm.

14. Dream work. The dream work is the process that turns the latent wish into the manifest dream, and it uses four tools. Condensation fuses several people or ideas into one image. Displacement moves emotional intensity from what matters onto something trivial. Symbolization puts a concrete image in place of a forbidden idea. Secondary revision is the waking mind smoothing the result into a story that seems coherent. Example: the boss in your dream has your father's voice and your brother's face, and you are furious about his tie. That is condensation (three men in one) plus displacement (the anger moved onto the tie). Interpreting a dream means running the dream work backward.

15. Parapraxes. You don't need to be asleep for the repressed to break through. Slips of the tongue, misreadings, misplaced objects, and forgotten names are small, successful intrusions of an unconscious intention into conscious action. A host says, "I'm so glad you could leave—I mean, come." He said what he meant, then corrected it. Forgetting an appointment with someone you resent works the same way. By psychic determinism, none of these is an accident. Parapraxes show that ordinary waking life runs on the same mechanisms as dreams and symptoms.

16. Free association and the fundamental rule. If the unconscious shows itself whenever censorship relaxes, then treatment should relax censorship deliberately. That is free association. The patient follows the fundamental rule: say everything that comes to mind, without exception, however trivial, embarrassing, or irrelevant it seems. Thoughts that look random are linked by unconscious connections, and following the chain leads back to what was hidden. The patient starts with a dentist appointment and, five associations later, is describing a childhood punishment they hadn't thought about in decades. The places where the rule is hard to follow, where the patient hesitates or changes the subject, are exactly where the material lies.

17. Transference. Free association brings up the past in words. Transference brings it up in feeling, aimed at the analyst. The patient experiences the analyst as a figure from their past and relives old love, fear, hostility, or need in the present. A patient becomes convinced the analyst disapproves of her, with the same cold disapproval her mother showed, even though the analyst has said nothing. Transference first looked like an obstacle. It turned out to be the treatment's main tool, because the old conflict now happens live, in the room, where it can be interpreted rather than only remembered.

18. Countertransference. The analyst has an unconscious too. Countertransference is the analyst's own emotional reaction to the patient. It was first treated as contamination. Contemporary practice uses it as information. If an analyst feels strangely bored with one patient and with no one else, that boredom may be the patient's deadened emotional life, transmitted and registered. The discipline lies in telling which feelings belong to the analyst's own history and which are the patient's inner world being felt from outside.

Conclusion. The hidden mind leaks out through dreams, slips, associations, and above all through the relationship with the analyst, and the analyst's own reactions become one more instrument for detecting it. Lecture 4 asks what happens once the hidden material is in the open.

LECTURE 3: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. An artist awaiting a competition result dreams that a judge begs her to accept an ordinary cake, which she refuses with satisfaction. A friend says the cake must symbolize money. Is that interpretation warranted? Offer a plausible reading of the wish and explain how you would decide between it and an alternative. (Answer: Distinguish the remembered scene from a hypothesized latent wish, such as reversing dependence on judgment or gaining power to refuse recognition. Connect the interpretation to the waiting situation and satisfaction while seeking the artist's associations. Reject a universal symbol code or certainty based only on the cake.)
2. A librarian dreams that a customer has her aunt's manner and her old coach's face. She feels intense rage about a chipped cup on the desk. Is the cup necessarily the real source of the anger? Explain a stronger possible interpretation and what associations would test it. (Answer: Consider condensation of different relationships in the customer and displacement of affect onto the cup. Explain why the mismatch between trivial object and strong feeling matters, while allowing a personally meaningful cup or a current conflict as alternatives. Interpretation requires the dreamer's associations, not simply naming mechanisms.)
3. A student congratulates a rival by saying, "Fortunately you failed," then immediately corrects himself to "finished." He insists it was only a verbal error. Should you accept that explanation, reject it, or reserve judgment? Defend your answer using both the rivalry and the limits of a single slip as evidence. (Answer: The rivalry makes an intrusive hostile wish plausible, but a slip alone does not prove it. Weigh context, prior feelings, recurrence, and ordinary speech error. A strong answer distinguishes a psychoanalytic hypothesis about conflicting intentions from an accusation treated as fact.)
4. During free association, a patient moves from a warehouse smell to a childhood song, pauses, and says the connection is too silly to mention. Should the analyst steer back to the warehouse, supply an interpretation, or invite the omitted association? Justify the intervention and explain what the hesitation might mean without assuming it proves a hidden event. (Answer: Prefer a non-leading invitation to continue the patient's own association because dismissing apparently irrelevant material may reproduce censorship. Consider the hesitation as possible resistance or embarrassment, not proof of trauma or of a specific memory. Explain why directing or supplying the missing content would compromise the inquiry.)
5. After a counselor reschedules one appointment, a patient becomes certain she will be abandoned, although the counselor offers a replacement time. The patient grew up with an unreliable caregiver. Is calling the reaction transference an adequate response to the counselor's actual change of plan? Assess both the historical pattern and the present relationship. (Answer: Link the intensity or expectation of abandonment to possible reactivation of an earlier relationship, while acknowledging the real disruption and the counselor's responsibility. Both past and present may contribute. A strong judgment neither dismisses the feeling as imaginary nor treats the historical parallel as proof that nothing happened now.)
6. A therapist feels compelled to rescue one apparently capable client and starts offering unusually extensive practical help. Is that impulse reliable information about the client's inner world, or a reason to question the therapist's own involvement? Explain how the therapist could distinguish these possibilities before acting further. (Answer: Treat countertransference as possible information and possible personal enactment. Compare reactions across clients, examine the therapist's own rescue patterns, and seek reflective supervision and evidence from the relationship. An intense feeling is not self-validating evidence that the client needs rescuing.)

LECTURE 4: CURE, CONFLICT, AND LOSS
Topics: resistance and working through; interpretation and insight; signal anxiety; narcissism and object relations; mourning and melancholia; ambivalence
========================================

Introduction. Finding the hidden wish is only half of treatment. The patient defends against discovery, insight takes time to change anything, and the conflicts that come to light have recognizable shapes. Today covers the mechanics of change and the conflicts it works on.

19. Resistance and working through. The same forces that caused repression oppose its undoing in treatment. That opposition is resistance. It is usually unconscious and takes ordinary forms: lateness, forgotten sessions, silence, sudden doubts about the analyst. A patient who arrives late to every session after an important memory surfaces is showing resistance, not bad timekeeping. Resistance means treatment is close to something. One insight doesn't end it. The patient must meet the same conflict again and again, in different situations and in the transference, before the old pattern gives way. That slow, repeated process is working through.

20. Interpretation and insight. The analyst's main intervention is interpretation: naming the hidden meaning behind what the patient says or does. A good interpretation comes when the material is near the surface and the patient can bear to hear it, and it connects present behavior to its unconscious source. "You fight with every boss the way you fought your father." When the patient grasps this as something lived and not just an idea, that is insight, and insight is the mechanism of cure. The symptom existed to keep a conflict out of awareness, so once the conflict is conscious, the symptom has no job left. Intellectual agreement doesn't count. The patient must recognize it with feeling.

21. Signal anxiety. Anxiety was first understood as dammed-up libido turning sour. The later view reverses this: anxiety is a signal. It is the ego's alarm that a dangerous wish is pushing toward awareness or action, and it triggers defense. Picture someone at a party who is suddenly uneasy at the exact moment harmless flirting turns toward a friend's spouse. The anxiety comes before any conscious recognition of the wish, and its job is to stop the wish before it arrives. The dangers it anticipates repeat those of childhood: losing the loved person, losing their love, punishment, and the superego's condemnation.

22. Narcissism and object relations. Libido can be invested in the self or in other people, whom psychoanalysis calls objects. Investing it in the self is narcissism. Some narcissism is normal and underlies self-respect. In excess, other people become mirrors and stop being separate persons. Picture someone who can love only partners who admire and reflect him, and who drops them when the mirror cracks. Object relations is the study of how we relate to others. Its central claim is that the earliest relationships leave internal templates that every later relationship is made to fit. You don't meet people fresh; you meet them through the internal figures you already carry.

23. Mourning, melancholia, and identification. Grief comes in two forms. In mourning, the bereaved person slowly withdraws attachment from the lost person, memory by memory, and is eventually free to love again. In melancholia, that withdrawal fails. The ego identifies with the lost person, taking them inside, and then turns against itself the reproaches it held against them. A widow attacks herself as selfish and cold, and those were exactly her complaints about her husband. The melancholic's self-hatred is aimed at the wrong target. Depression can be savage because the person has become the battleground for an unfinished quarrel with someone who is gone.

24. Ambivalence. Underneath many of these conflicts is ambivalence: love and hate directed at the same person at the same time. It is unavoidable toward anyone we depend on, because the person who gratifies us is also the one who frustrates us. Ambivalence becomes pathological when one side is repressed. A son devotedly nurses his dying father and dreams of his funeral. The love is conscious and the hostility goes underground, where it returns as guilt, symptoms, or the excessive devotion of reaction formation.

Conclusion. Treatment is a slow fight against resistance, carried by interpretations that turn into felt insight. The conflicts it uncovers have recurring forms: anxiety as an alarm, self-love that crowds out other people, grief that turns on the self, and love divided against itself. Lecture 5 widens the view beyond the individual.

LECTURE 4: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. After acknowledging anger toward her mother, a patient begins bringing elaborate charts of her moods to every session but avoids discussing any angry encounter. Is the extra effort evidence of progress, resistance, or both? Explain your judgment and what working through would require beyond producing more charts. (Answer: Consider conscientious participation that also keeps threatening feeling at an intellectual distance. Judge the function of the charts rather than equating activity with progress or resistance. Working through would involve repeatedly recognizing and experiencing the conflict in actual relationships, including the analytic one, rather than merely documenting it.)
2. A patient fluently explains that a sibling's departure shaped his fear of a partner leaving, yet keeps accusing the partner whenever they spend an evening apart. He says knowing the explanation means the problem is resolved. Do you agree? Distinguish a useful intellectual understanding from a change that counts as lived insight. (Answer: Acknowledge that the explanation may be useful while using the continuing pattern to question whether the conflict has been emotionally recognized and worked through. Explain what changed experience, tolerance of separation, or behavior would support deeper insight. Do not promise that a single emotional realization guarantees a cure.)
3. An employee feels a sudden wave of anxiety just before accepting a promotion and then jokes that she never wanted advancement. Her workplace also has a history of retaliating against successful women. Is signal anxiety about an internal conflict the best explanation? Weigh it against realistic danger and explain what would settle the balance. (Answer: Consider an internal alarm about rivalry, guilt, or forbidden ambition and the defensive retreat from desire, but take the actual workplace risk seriously. Distinguish anticipated external harm from superego condemnation or loss of love using contextual evidence. A mixed explanation can be stronger than reducing all anxiety to unconscious wishes.)
4. A novelist cuts off a long-standing friend after the friend misses one book launch to care for an ill parent. The novelist says, "If she valued me, she would have been there." Is this ordinary hurt, a failure to recognize the friend's separateness, or both? Give a proportionate interpretation and identify what a broader relationship history could change. (Answer: Consider the demand for the friend to function as a source of admiration rather than an independent person with competing needs. Ground that interpretation in the response to the emergency while allowing disappointment without a global diagnosis. The repeated pattern, capacity for repair, and treatment of other relationships matter.)
5. After a band breaks up, its bassist repeatedly calls himself disloyal and unreliable, using the same words he once directed at the singer who left. Is this realistic self-criticism or a possible melancholic response to loss? Explain how you would adjudicate without dismissing genuine responsibility. (Answer: Consider identification with the lost person and reproaches turned against the self, supported by the repeated language and timing after the loss. Compare actual behavior and proportionate responsibility with global self-attack. Distinguish this hypothesis from ordinary regret, and do not infer a clinical diagnosis from the short account.)
6. A mother proudly supports her daughter's scholarship abroad but repeatedly makes errors in the travel arrangements and feels ashamed of brief relief when a flight is cancelled. Does this show she does not love her daughter? Offer a better account of the mixed evidence and explain what would keep it from becoming a simplistic accusation of sabotage. (Answer: Explain how love can coexist with anger, fear of separation, or a wish to keep a loved person close. Relate the mixed feelings to possible ambivalence without equating it with absence of love. Practical mistakes and relief have other causes, so conscious intent to sabotage is not established.)

LECTURE 5: BEYOND THE INDIVIDUAL
Topics: the death drive and repetition compulsion; civilization and renunciation; the unconscious in everyday culture; post-Freudian revisions; the analytic frame; contemporary listening
========================================

Introduction. The first four lectures treated the individual mind. This one moves outward: to a drive that seems to work against life, to the price civilization charges, to culture as an outlet for the repressed, to the theorists who rebuilt the theory, and to the setting where the work is done.

25. The death drive and repetition compulsion. Some behavior defies the pleasure principle. People repeat painful experiences over and over. Trauma survivors relive the event in dreams, and a woman leaves one abusive partner only to choose the same kind of man again. This repetition compulsion points to a force more basic than the pursuit of pleasure. Freud's answer was the death drive, a tendency in living matter to return to an inanimate state. Turned inward, it appears as self-destruction. Turned outward, it becomes aggression. The death drive is the most disputed idea in psychoanalysis. The observation behind it is not in dispute, and any theory of mind has to account for it.

26. Civilization and its cost. Civilization requires giving up drive satisfaction. We cannot act on every sexual or aggressive impulse, and social life depends on not doing so. The renounced drives don't disappear. Aggression that can't go outward is turned inward and taken over by the superego, which uses it against the ego as guilt. The more civilized a society, the harsher its internal policing and the more pervasive its discontent. This is why orderly, prosperous societies are not happy ones: their comfort is paid for in chronic guilt and anxiety.

27. The unconscious in everyday culture. Repressed wishes produce culture as well as symptoms. A joke lets forbidden aggression or sexuality through in a form everyone can enjoy. The employee who mocks the boss with a clever line gets the insult out, and the laughter is the shared release of the energy that normally holds it back. Art works the same way, through sublimation and disguise, giving private fantasies a form others can share. Religion recreates the childhood situation: an all-powerful father, protection in return for obedience, guilt for transgression. Culture is where the repressed is allowed to return in acceptable form.

28. Post-Freudian revisions. Four major figures rebuilt the theory. Jung added a collective unconscious, shared by everyone and populated by archetypes such as the Mother, the Shadow, and the Hero. Adler made inferiority and the striving for power, not sexuality, the central motive. Klein traced mental life to the infant's earliest fantasies about the mother, split into all-good and all-bad part-objects. Lacan held that the unconscious is structured like a language, so that symptoms are signifiers to be read. Take one dream of a devouring mother: for Jung it is an archetype, for Adler a power struggle, for Klein a persecutory part-object, for Lacan a chain of signifiers. Judge between them by asking which reading explains the most of the patient's material with the fewest assumptions.

29. The analytic setting and frame. The frame consists of fixed session times, a fixed fee, a set frequency, the couch, and the analyst's neutrality. These aren't bureaucracy. They create a stable container in which unconscious material can unfold, and against which deviations stand out. Because the frame is constant, every challenge to it becomes material for analysis. A patient who keeps trying to renegotiate the fee is enacting something, perhaps about worth, debt, or specialness. Analyzing why the patient wants to break the frame often yields more than anything said inside it.

30. Psychoanalytic listening today. Contemporary practice keeps the core method and broadens the field of attention. The analyst listens to what is said, to what is avoided, to how it is said, and to the feelings that come up in the room. A patient breezily describes a breakup while twisting her ring the entire session, and the analyst attends to the gap between the words and the hands. This listening uses everything in this course: nothing is noise, the relationship is data, and the breezy tone is a defense doing work.

Conclusion. The course has followed one idea from its beginning to its widest reach. The mind is split against itself, hides wishes from itself, and pays for the hiding in dreams, slips, symptoms, and suffering. The same mechanisms shape jokes, art, religion, and civilization. The final oral exam asks you to use these ideas on cases. Reciting definitions won't be enough; you'll have to interpret.

LECTURE 5: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. A founder repeatedly chooses domineering business partners, leaves in distress, and then recruits someone strikingly similar. She also works in an industry where those partners control most funding. Is repetition compulsion the strongest explanation, or does it overlook important circumstances? Defend a judgment and explain why the pattern would not by itself establish the death drive. (Answer: Recognize a repeated painful relational pattern while weighing restricted options, material incentives, and familiar expectations. Distinguish the observation of repetition from Freud's disputed explanation of it by the death drive. Avoid blaming the person or treating recurrence as proof of a wish to suffer.)
2. A union treasurer never expresses anger toward colleagues but viciously condemns herself over tiny accounting errors. Is this simply a strong conscience, or could social restraint have an internal cost? Use the course's account of civilization to assess the disproportion, and explain what evidence could support another explanation. (Answer: Consider aggression barred from outward expression becoming an internal punitive demand, connecting suppressed anger and disproportionate self-attack. Contrast this with realistic accountability, anxiety, or other sources of guilt. Applying the social account does not establish that all conscience or guilt comes from inward aggression.)
3. Tenants who never openly challenge a domineering landlord enthusiastically share a comic sketch making him ridiculous. After laughing, some feel less inclined to organize a complaint. Does the humor merely entertain, or might it also release and contain conflict? Adjudicate using the case, including what the change in behavior adds to your interpretation. (Answer: Explain how disguised or socially permitted aggression may gain expression and shared release through the sketch. Consider its possible tension-reducing function and its possible effect on collective action without assuming all jokes have the same motive. Connect the judgment to the avoidance of direct challenge and the response afterward.)
4. A graduate dreams of standing on a locked stage while an audience waits. Her associations concern being outperformed by classmates and feeling worthwhile only when she wins. An Adlerian reading emphasizes inferiority and striving; a Jungian reading emphasizes an archetypal ordeal. Which is better supported here, and what new material might make you favor the other? (Answer: Compare what each reading explains with the fewest additional assumptions, using the graduate's own associations as evidence rather than choosing by theorist's name. The inferiority reading has direct support, but a reasoned alternative is acceptable if justified. Specify material that could strengthen the archetypal account and keep the verdict provisional.)
5. A patient asks to move every session to a different cafe and says the consulting room makes him feel ranked below the therapist. He also mentions that the room's lighting causes headaches. Should the request be interpreted as a challenge to the analytic frame, accommodated as a practical need, or approached in both ways? Explain your decision. (Answer: Consider the relational meaning of rank, dependence, or special arrangements while taking the reported physical discomfort seriously. Explore the request without assuming every proposed change is resistance and consider reasonable accommodation within a stable frame. A useful interpretation must account for both practical and emotional evidence.)
6. A researcher cheerfully calls a dismissal "the best thing that ever happened," keeps folding and unfolding an old lab coat, and avoids saying the supervisor's name. Should the analyst accept the upbeat account, conclude that the body reveals the truth, or explore the discrepancy? Justify a response that uses more than the spoken words without pretending to read the mind. (Answer: Attend to words, avoidance, actions, affect, and the interaction, and invite the patient's own meaning for the discrepancy. Consider relief alongside hurt, anger, or defended loss. Neither literal acceptance nor a confident diagnosis from a gesture is sufficient; interpretations should remain open to correction.)

FINAL ORAL EXAM
Five questions. The AI examiner may ask one follow-up per question when an answer is vague.
========================================

1. A patient warmly thanks an analyst for helping him recognize resentment toward a dependent brother. He then sends unusually meticulous summaries before sessions but repeatedly steers away from discussing the brother. Is this cooperation, resistance, or a mixture? Adjudicate using at least three course concepts and propose a non-leading next step.
A strong answer covers: the function of apparent cooperation, possible resistance and repression, intellectual understanding versus felt insight or working through, and potentially transference. Explain the details rather than listing concepts; allow a supported mixed judgment and an open invitation to explore what is avoided.

2. A designer denied credit for a project dreams that a public exhibition awards its prize to a nameless apprentice wearing the director's watch. The designer wakes pleased and associates the apprentice with herself and a younger sister. Does a wish-fulfillment interpretation explain the dream better than a literal prediction of the exhibition result? Defend your reading and say what would weaken it.
A strong answer covers: manifest scene versus a plausible latent wish for recognition or reversal, condensation or displacement grounded in the associations, and the significance and limits of the pleased reaction. Compare interpretations without using fixed symbols or treating the dream as proof of one hidden wish.

3. Two people resent a colleague's success. One becomes extravagantly solicitous toward the colleague while remaining tense; the other channels competitive energy into building a useful training program and becomes less preoccupied with the rivalry. Are these the same kind of resolution? Adjudicate the differences and explain what further evidence would challenge your reading.
A strong answer covers: a possible reversal of hostility into excessive concern in reaction formation contrasted with possible redirection and satisfaction through sublimation. Apply both to the details, distinguish actual generosity from defensive excess, and allow evidence that the constructive activity may itself serve another defensive purpose.

4. An adult who wants independence from a demanding parent develops intense guilt and repeated headaches whenever a move away becomes possible. Cancelling the move brings immediate relief but deep resentment. A friend calls this mere indecision. Is a compromise-formation account more explanatory? Adjudicate using the structural model, while addressing what the vignette cannot establish.
A strong answer covers: the wish for autonomy, internal prohibitions or fear of losing love, and a possible ego solution that blocks separation while indirectly expressing the conflict. Connect relief and resentment to the competing demands, consider practical and medical alternatives, and do not assume that headaches have a psychological origin.

5. A successful apprentice repeatedly abandons projects just before independent recognition, saying that success would make a beloved mentor unnecessary. The mentor actively discourages independence. Compare a Freudian account of conflict and repetition with one post-Freudian account, then judge which explains more of this case without ignoring the mentor's actual conduct.
A strong answer covers: a grounded Freudian reading involving conflicting wishes, guilt, dependence, or repetition, compared with a supported account such as internal object relations or Adlerian inferiority and striving. Weigh the actual relational pressure, explain the verdict using case evidence, and identify missing evidence rather than treating one theory as automatically correct.

LECTURE QUESTIONS (30 questions, 40% of final grade)
Score each oral answer 0 to 2:
Every question presents a fresh situation. Adjudicate it using the lecture's ideas; do not merely recall a lecture example or name a concept.
2 = Gives a defensible judgment, applies the relevant concepts to specific case details, and supports the reasoning, including alternatives or limits when the question calls for them.
1 = Shows some relevant understanding but the application or justification is incomplete or vague; naming the correct concept alone earns at most 1.
0 = Gives no relevant case-based reasoning, seriously misapplies the concepts, or gives no answer.

Score each answer 0 to 4:

1 = Uses the terms without showing understanding.
0 = Wrong, or no substantive answer.
