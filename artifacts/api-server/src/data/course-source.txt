BASIC TENETS OF PSYCHOANALYSIS
An Oral Course

FORMAT
This is an oral class. The AI lecturer delivers each lecture by voice. After each lecture, the AI asks that lecture's six questions aloud, and the student answers orally in one or two sentences. Each answer is scored on the spot. After Lecture 5, the AI conducts the final oral exam.

========================================
LECTURE 1: THE HIDDEN MIND
Topics: the unconscious; psychic determinism; repression; drives; the pleasure and reality principles; id, ego, superego
========================================

Introduction. Psychoanalysis starts from one claim: you know far less about your own mind than you think. The reasons you give for your actions are a small, filtered part of what is actually happening in you. Today's six ideas form a single argument. Behavior outruns conscious reasons, so there is an unconscious. Nothing mental is random, so the unconscious can be traced. Some contents are actively hidden, which is repression. What gets hidden are wishes, which come from drives. Drives meet reality under two principles. The mind manages all of this through three agencies.

1. The unconscious. Common sense says the mind is transparent to itself: if I want something, I know it. This is false. A hypnotized subject is told he will open an umbrella indoors when the clock strikes. He wakes with no memory of the instruction, opens the umbrella on cue, and when asked why, invents a reason, such as checking it for holes. The real cause was a mental state he could not access. The same thing happens without hypnosis. A man swears he is over his ex, yet takes the route past her apartment every day. Some unconscious content is merely unattended. The important kind is dynamically unconscious: it is kept from awareness because knowing it would hurt. We infer it the way we infer anyone's mind, from behavior that only that state would explain.

2. Psychic determinism. No physical event is uncaused, and psychoanalysis applies the same rule to the mind: no mental event is accidental. Slips, forgettings, and "random" choices all have causes, usually motives. Ask someone for a random number and have them associate to it, and it routinely turns out to be an address, an age, or a parent's death date. As a result, everything counts as evidence, and nothing a person says or does can be dismissed as noise. Most mental events are overdetermined: several causes converge on them, so a single slip can carry more than one meaning.

3. Repression. Why are certain things unconscious? Not through ordinary forgetting. Repression is motivated exclusion. The mind pushes threatening content out of awareness and spends continuous effort keeping it out. The signs are selectivity and resistance. A woman remembers every childhood summer except the one when a family friend lived in the house, and she grows uneasy when questioned about it. Because keeping something repressed takes effort, the repressed returns whenever that effort slackens: in dreams, fatigue, intoxication, and symptoms. Distinguish this from suppression, which is the conscious decision not to think about something. Repression happens without the person knowing they are doing it.

4. Drives. What gets repressed is wishes, and wishes come from drives. A drive has a bodily source, a pressure, an aim (satisfaction), and an object (whatever happens to satisfy it). The object is the most variable part, which is why drives can be redirected, disguised, and displaced. The two great drives are sexual, understood broadly as the pursuit of pleasure and attachment, and aggressive. Drives are the engine of mental life. Without them there would be nothing to repress and no conflict. Their force shows when they are blocked: restless irritability that disappears the moment a suppressed desire is acknowledged.

5. The pleasure principle and the reality principle. The infant mind runs on the pleasure principle. It seeks immediate discharge of tension and, when frustrated, even hallucinates satisfaction. Hallucination does not feed anyone, so reality forces a second principle. The reality principle introduces delay, planning, and thought, which is essentially trial action carried out in the head. A toddler grabs the cookie; a ten-year-old waits until after dinner and gets two. The reality principle does not abolish the pleasure principle. It serves it more effectively. The pleasure principle keeps a protected reserve in daydreams and fantasy, where reality never has to be consulted.

6. Id, ego, superego. The mind is organized into three agencies. The id is the reservoir of drives. It is illogical, timeless, and tolerant of contradiction, and it demands satisfaction now. The ego perceives reality, tests it, delays action, and builds defenses, and much of its work is itself unconscious. The superego is internalized parental authority: the conscience that forbids and the ideal that demands, often harsher than the actual parents were. In a single morning you can see all three. You want to skip work (id), you feel guilty at the thought (superego), and you call in for a half day (ego). The ego serves three masters: the id, the superego, and reality. Mental health is the ego managing that negotiation. Neurosis is the ego failing at it.

Conclusion. Put the pieces together and you have the psychoanalytic picture of the mind. It is driven by wishes, split against itself, and actively hiding parts of itself from itself. Yet every part can be traced, because nothing in it is accidental. Lecture 2 maps where hidden contents sit, how the drives develop through childhood, and how these conflicts produce defenses and symptoms.

LECTURE 1: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. In the example of the man and his ex, what reveals his hidden motive? (Answer: His choice of route past her apartment.)
2. Are any mental events truly accidental? (Answer: No. Every mental event has a cause.)
3. How does repression differ from ordinary forgetting? (Answer: It is motivated and active. The mind works to keep the content out.)
4. Name the two main drives. (Answer: Sexual and aggressive.)
5. What does the reality principle add to the pleasure principle? (Answer: Delay, planning, and thought.)
6. Which agency mediates between the id, the superego, and reality? (Answer: The ego.)

LECTURE 2: MAPS, STAGES, AND DEFENSES
Topics: the topographic model; infantile sexuality; the Oedipus complex; fixation and regression; defense mechanisms; symptom formation
========================================

Introduction. Lecture 1 showed that the mind hides wishes from itself. Today we ask three questions. Where do hidden contents sit? Where do the wishes come from developmentally? What does the mind do to keep them hidden? The answers lead to the central result of this lecture: a symptom is not a malfunction but a solution, and a bad one.

7. The topographic model. Before the structural model came a simpler map with three regions. The conscious is whatever you are aware of right now. The preconscious holds what you can retrieve on demand, like your phone number or yesterday's lunch. The unconscious holds what is barred from awareness and cannot be retrieved at will, such as a forbidden childhood wish. The important border is the censorship between the unconscious and the preconscious. The map's weakness is that the censor itself works unconsciously, which is why the structural model later supplemented it. Use the topographic model for where a content is. Use the structural model for who wants it and who opposes it.

8. Infantile sexuality and the stages. The most resisted claim in psychoanalysis is that sexuality begins in infancy. "Sexual" here means the pursuit of bodily pleasure in general, not adult genital sex. That pursuit centers on different zones in a fixed order. In the oral stage, pleasure comes from sucking and feeding. In the anal stage, it comes from retaining and expelling, and so from control; a toddler's pride and stubbornness over toilet training is the clearest example. In the phallic stage, pleasure focuses on the genitals and on curiosity about sexual difference. Latency follows, a quiet period in which these interests are set aside. Genital sexuality arrives at puberty and gathers the earlier pleasures into adult form. Each stage leaves a residue in character, and adult personality is in large part the history of these stages.

9. The Oedipus complex and conscience. In the phallic stage the child wants exclusive possession of one parent and treats the other as a rival. This produces wishes the child cannot act on and fears punishment for. The resolution is decisive: the child gives up the rivalry and identifies with the rival, becoming like the parent instead of replacing them. With that identification, the parent's prohibitions move inside. A boy stops competing with his father, starts imitating him, and now hears his father's rules as his own voice. This is how the superego forms. Conscience is not innate. It is an inheritance from a conflict, and that is why it can be cruel: it carries the force of the aggression that was turned away from the parent.

10. Fixation and regression. When a stage brings too much frustration or too much gratification, part of the libido stays attached there. That is a fixation. Fixations are weak points. Under later stress, the person regresses: they fall back to the satisfactions of the stage where they are fixated. After a layoff, a man starts chain-eating and chewing pens. That is oral regression, a return to the earliest source of comfort. The stress does not create the regression; it follows a path laid down in childhood. The fixation point also predicts the form of the regression. An anally fixated person under stress becomes rigid, hoarding, and controlling, not hungry.

11. Defense mechanisms. When a forbidden wish nears awareness, the ego defends itself, unconsciously. Denial refuses the fact itself: "I'm not an alcoholic." Projection puts your own impulse into someone else: I hate him becomes he hates me. Reaction formation adopts the opposite of the impulse, as in the crusader against homosexuality who has same-sex desires. The excess is the tell. Sublimation redirects the impulse toward a valued aim, as when aggression becomes surgery. It is the only defense that actually discharges the drive and doesn't just block it. Rationalization puts a respectable reason in place of the real one: "I didn't want that job anyway." Every defense trades truth for comfort.

12. Symptom formation. When defenses fail, symptoms form, and a symptom is a compromise. It expresses the forbidden wish and the defense against it at the same time. Take a hand paralysis with no physical cause, in a man who wanted to strike his hated father. The paralysis enacts the aggression, since the hand is now the center of his life, and blocks it, since the hand cannot strike. Both sides of the conflict get partial satisfaction, and that is why symptoms are so stubborn: removing one means reopening the conflict it settled. Treatment does not attack the symptom. It finds the conflict the symptom is resolving.

Conclusion. Childhood drives pass through stages and leave fixations. The Oedipal conflict installs a conscience. Forbidden wishes are held down by defenses, and when the defenses fail, the result is a symptom that half-satisfies everyone. Lecture 3 turns to the places where hidden wishes surface: dreams, slips, free association, and the relationship with the analyst.

LECTURE 2: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. Where is your phone number until you recall it? (Answer: In the preconscious.)
2. Which developmental stage does toilet training belong to? (Answer: The anal stage.)
3. How is the Oedipus complex resolved? (Answer: By identifying with the rival parent, which forms the superego.)
4. What triggers regression, and where does it lead? (Answer: Stress. It leads back to the fixation point.)
5. Which defense mechanism is "sour grapes"? (Answer: Rationalization.)
6. What two things does a symptom combine? (Answer: The forbidden wish and the defense against it.)

LECTURE 3: WHERE THE HIDDEN SURFACES
Topics: dreams as wish fulfillment; dream work; parapraxes; free association; transference; countertransference
========================================

Introduction. The repressed does not stay quiet. It returns wherever the ego's guard drops: in sleep, in slips, and in the room with the analyst. Today we look at those openings and at the method built to exploit them.

13. Dreams as wish fulfillment. Every dream is the disguised fulfillment of a wish. What you remember, the plot and the images, is the manifest content. What it means, the wish it fulfills, is the latent content. The disguise exists because the censor still works during sleep, only at reduced strength. The wish gets through, but only in costume. A dream about attending a stranger's funeral can carry a wish for a rival's death. Anxiety dreams are not counterexamples. They are cases where the disguise failed and the wish came too close, so the censor sounded the alarm.

14. Dream work. The dream work is the process that turns the latent wish into the manifest dream, and it uses four tools. Condensation fuses several people or ideas into one image. Displacement moves emotional intensity from what matters onto something trivial. Symbolization puts a concrete image in place of a forbidden idea. Secondary revision is the waking mind smoothing the result into a story that seems coherent. Example: the boss in your dream has your father's voice and your brother's face, and you are furious about his tie. That is condensation (three men in one) plus displacement (the anger moved onto the tie). Interpreting a dream means running the dream work backward.

15. Parapraxes. You don't need to be asleep for the repressed to break through. Slips of the tongue, misreadings, misplaced objects, and forgotten names are small, successful intrusions of an unconscious intention into conscious action. A host says, "I'm so glad you could leave—I mean, come." He said what he meant, then corrected it. Forgetting an appointment with someone you resent works the same way. By psychic determinism, none of these is an accident. Parapraxes show that ordinary waking life runs on the same mechanisms as dreams and symptoms.

16. Free association and the fundamental rule. If the unconscious shows itself whenever censorship relaxes, then treatment should relax censorship deliberately. That is free association. The patient follows the fundamental rule: say everything that comes to mind, without exception, however trivial, embarrassing, or irrelevant it seems. Thoughts that look random are linked by unconscious connections, and following the chain leads back to what was hidden. The patient starts with a dentist appointment and, five associations later, is describing a childhood punishment they hadn't thought about in decades. The places where the rule is hard to follow, where the patient hesitates or changes the subject, are exactly where the material lies.

17. Transference. Free association brings up the past in words. Transference brings it up in feeling, aimed at the analyst. The patient experiences the analyst as a figure from their past and relives old love, fear, hostility, or need in the present. A patient becomes convinced the analyst disapproves of her, with the same cold disapproval her mother showed, even though the analyst has said nothing. Transference first looked like an obstacle. It turned out to be the treatment's main tool, because the old conflict now happens live, in the room, where it can be interpreted rather than only remembered.

18. Countertransference. The analyst has an unconscious too. Countertransference is the analyst's own emotional reaction to the patient. It was first treated as contamination. Contemporary practice uses it as information. If an analyst feels strangely bored with one patient and with no one else, that boredom may be the patient's deadened emotional life, transmitted and registered. The discipline lies in telling which feelings belong to the analyst's own history and which are the patient's inner world being felt from outside.

Conclusion. The hidden mind leaks out through dreams, slips, associations, and above all through the relationship with the analyst, and the analyst's own reactions become one more instrument for detecting it. Lecture 4 asks what happens once the hidden material is in the open.

LECTURE 3: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. What is the hidden meaning of a dream called? (Answer: The latent content.)
2. In a dream, anger aimed at a trivial detail is an example of which mechanism? (Answer: Displacement.)
3. What does a slip of the tongue reveal? (Answer: An unconscious intention.)
4. What does the fundamental rule require of the patient? (Answer: To say everything that comes to mind, without censoring.)
5. In transference, toward whom are feelings about past figures redirected? (Answer: The analyst.)
6. Is countertransference only an obstacle? (Answer: No. It is information about the patient.)

LECTURE 4: CURE, CONFLICT, AND LOSS
Topics: resistance and working through; interpretation and insight; signal anxiety; narcissism and object relations; mourning and melancholia; ambivalence
========================================

Introduction. Finding the hidden wish is only half of treatment. The patient defends against discovery, insight takes time to change anything, and the conflicts that come to light have recognizable shapes. Today covers the mechanics of change and the conflicts it works on.

19. Resistance and working through. The same forces that caused repression oppose its undoing in treatment. That opposition is resistance. It is usually unconscious and takes ordinary forms: lateness, forgotten sessions, silence, sudden doubts about the analyst. A patient who arrives late to every session after an important memory surfaces is showing resistance, not bad timekeeping. Resistance means treatment is close to something. One insight doesn't end it. The patient must meet the same conflict again and again, in different situations and in the transference, before the old pattern gives way. That slow, repeated process is working through.

20. Interpretation and insight. The analyst's main intervention is interpretation: naming the hidden meaning behind what the patient says or does. A good interpretation comes when the material is near the surface and the patient can bear to hear it, and it connects present behavior to its unconscious source. "You fight with every boss the way you fought your father." When the patient grasps this as something lived and not just an idea, that is insight, and insight is the mechanism of cure. The symptom existed to keep a conflict out of awareness, so once the conflict is conscious, the symptom has no job left. Intellectual agreement doesn't count. The patient must recognize it with feeling.

21. Signal anxiety. Anxiety was first understood as dammed-up libido turning sour. The later view reverses this: anxiety is a signal. It is the ego's alarm that a dangerous wish is pushing toward awareness or action, and it triggers defense. Picture someone at a party who is suddenly uneasy at the exact moment harmless flirting turns toward a friend's spouse. The anxiety comes before any conscious recognition of the wish, and its job is to stop the wish before it arrives. The dangers it anticipates repeat those of childhood: losing the loved person, losing their love, punishment, and the superego's condemnation.

22. Narcissism and object relations. Libido can be invested in the self or in other people, whom psychoanalysis calls objects. Investing it in the self is narcissism. Some narcissism is normal and underlies self-respect. In excess, other people become mirrors and stop being separate persons. Picture someone who can love only partners who admire and reflect him, and who drops them when the mirror cracks. Object relations is the study of how we relate to others. Its central claim is that the earliest relationships leave internal templates that every later relationship is made to fit. You don't meet people fresh; you meet them through the internal figures you already carry.

23. Mourning, melancholia, and identification. Grief comes in two forms. In mourning, the bereaved person slowly withdraws attachment from the lost person, memory by memory, and is eventually free to love again. In melancholia, that withdrawal fails. The ego identifies with the lost person, taking them inside, and then turns against itself the reproaches it held against them. A widow attacks herself as selfish and cold, and those were exactly her complaints about her husband. The melancholic's self-hatred is aimed at the wrong target. Depression can be savage because the person has become the battleground for an unfinished quarrel with someone who is gone.

24. Ambivalence. Underneath many of these conflicts is ambivalence: love and hate directed at the same person at the same time. It is unavoidable toward anyone we depend on, because the person who gratifies us is also the one who frustrates us. Ambivalence becomes pathological when one side is repressed. A son devotedly nurses his dying father and dreams of his funeral. The love is conscious and the hostility goes underground, where it returns as guilt, symptoms, or the excessive devotion of reaction formation.

Conclusion. Treatment is a slow fight against resistance, carried by interpretations that turn into felt insight. The conflicts it uncovers have recurring forms: anxiety as an alarm, self-love that crowds out other people, grief that turns on the self, and love divided against itself. Lecture 5 widens the view beyond the individual.

LECTURE 4: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. What is working through? (Answer: Repeatedly confronting the same conflict until the old pattern changes.)
2. What is the mechanism of cure in psychoanalysis? (Answer: Insight, recognized with feeling.)
3. What does signal anxiety warn of? (Answer: A dangerous wish approaching awareness or action.)
4. What is libido invested in the self called? (Answer: Narcissism.)
5. In melancholia, whom does the patient reproach, and whom are the reproaches really about? (Answer: The patient reproaches themselves; the reproaches are really about the lost person.)
6. What two feelings coexist in ambivalence? (Answer: Love and hate, toward the same person.)

LECTURE 5: BEYOND THE INDIVIDUAL
Topics: the death drive and repetition compulsion; civilization and renunciation; the unconscious in everyday culture; post-Freudian revisions; the analytic frame; contemporary listening
========================================

Introduction. The first four lectures treated the individual mind. This one moves outward: to a drive that seems to work against life, to the price civilization charges, to culture as an outlet for the repressed, to the theorists who rebuilt the theory, and to the setting where the work is done.

25. The death drive and repetition compulsion. Some behavior defies the pleasure principle. People repeat painful experiences over and over. Trauma survivors relive the event in dreams, and a woman leaves one abusive partner only to choose the same kind of man again. This repetition compulsion points to a force more basic than the pursuit of pleasure. Freud's answer was the death drive, a tendency in living matter to return to an inanimate state. Turned inward, it appears as self-destruction. Turned outward, it becomes aggression. The death drive is the most disputed idea in psychoanalysis. The observation behind it is not in dispute, and any theory of mind has to account for it.

26. Civilization and its cost. Civilization requires giving up drive satisfaction. We cannot act on every sexual or aggressive impulse, and social life depends on not doing so. The renounced drives don't disappear. Aggression that can't go outward is turned inward and taken over by the superego, which uses it against the ego as guilt. The more civilized a society, the harsher its internal policing and the more pervasive its discontent. This is why orderly, prosperous societies are not happy ones: their comfort is paid for in chronic guilt and anxiety.

27. The unconscious in everyday culture. Repressed wishes produce culture as well as symptoms. A joke lets forbidden aggression or sexuality through in a form everyone can enjoy. The employee who mocks the boss with a clever line gets the insult out, and the laughter is the shared release of the energy that normally holds it back. Art works the same way, through sublimation and disguise, giving private fantasies a form others can share. Religion recreates the childhood situation: an all-powerful father, protection in return for obedience, guilt for transgression. Culture is where the repressed is allowed to return in acceptable form.

28. Post-Freudian revisions. Four major figures rebuilt the theory. Jung added a collective unconscious, shared by everyone and populated by archetypes such as the Mother, the Shadow, and the Hero. Adler made inferiority and the striving for power, not sexuality, the central motive. Klein traced mental life to the infant's earliest fantasies about the mother, split into all-good and all-bad part-objects. Lacan held that the unconscious is structured like a language, so that symptoms are signifiers to be read. Take one dream of a devouring mother: for Jung it is an archetype, for Adler a power struggle, for Klein a persecutory part-object, for Lacan a chain of signifiers. Judge between them by asking which reading explains the most of the patient's material with the fewest assumptions.

29. The analytic setting and frame. The frame consists of fixed session times, a fixed fee, a set frequency, the couch, and the analyst's neutrality. These aren't bureaucracy. They create a stable container in which unconscious material can unfold, and against which deviations stand out. Because the frame is constant, every challenge to it becomes material for analysis. A patient who keeps trying to renegotiate the fee is enacting something, perhaps about worth, debt, or specialness. Analyzing why the patient wants to break the frame often yields more than anything said inside it.

30. Psychoanalytic listening today. Contemporary practice keeps the core method and broadens the field of attention. The analyst listens to what is said, to what is avoided, to how it is said, and to the feelings that come up in the room. A patient breezily describes a breakup while twisting her ring the entire session, and the analyst attends to the gap between the words and the hands. This listening uses everything in this course: nothing is noise, the relationship is data, and the breezy tone is a defense doing work.

Conclusion. The course has followed one idea from its beginning to its widest reach. The mind is split against itself, hides wishes from itself, and pays for the hiding in dreams, slips, symptoms, and suffering. The same mechanisms shape jokes, art, religion, and civilization. The final oral exam asks you to use these ideas on cases. Reciting definitions won't be enough; you'll have to interpret.

LECTURE 5: ORAL QUESTIONS
1. What does the repetition compulsion repeat? (Answer: Painful experiences.)
2. What does civilization demand of the individual, and at what cost? (Answer: Renunciation of the drives, at the cost of guilt and discontent.)
3. What does a joke allow? (Answer: The expression of forbidden wishes in acceptable form.)
4. Who held that the unconscious is structured like a language? (Answer: Lacan.)
5. Why are a patient's challenges to the frame analyzed? (Answer: Because they reveal unconscious material.)
6. Besides what the patient says, what does contemporary listening attend to? (Answer: What is avoided, how it is said, and the feelings in the room.)

FINAL ORAL EXAM
Five questions. The AI examiner may ask one follow-up per question when an answer is vague.
========================================

1. Scenario: A woman forgets her therapy appointment the day after telling her analyst about her father's affair. Explain what happened, using at least three concepts from the course.
A strong answer covers: parapraxis, resistance, repression, and possibly transference.

2. Scenario: A man dreams he is calmly repairing a stranger's car, which then explodes. He wakes up feeling oddly satisfied. Distinguish the manifest content from a plausible latent content, and name the dream-work mechanisms involved.
A strong answer covers: the manifest content versus a hidden aggressive wish, displacement onto the stranger, and the satisfaction as a sign of wish fulfillment.

3. Compare: How do reaction formation and sublimation differ in what they do with a forbidden impulse? Give one example of each.
A strong answer covers: reaction formation reverses the impulse into its opposite, while sublimation redirects it toward a valued aim.

4. Integrate: Using the id, ego, and superego, explain how a symptom can be a compromise. Construct your own example.
A strong answer covers: the id's wish, the superego's prohibition, and the ego's solution that partially satisfies both. The example must show the symptom both expressing and blocking the wish.

5. Compare interpretations: A patient repeatedly enters relationships with cold, rejecting partners. Give the Freudian explanation, then one post-Freudian explanation, and say which is stronger and why.
A strong answer covers: repetition compulsion and transference of an early relationship, then for example Klein's internal objects or Adler's inferiority. The verdict must be justified, not just asserted.

GRADING RUBRIC
========================================

LECTURE QUESTIONS (30 questions, 40% of final grade)
Score each oral answer 0 to 2:
2 = Correct, and uses the concept accurately.
1 = Partially correct or vague.
0 = Wrong, or no answer.
The maximum per lecture is 12 points. The student needs 8 of 12 to unlock the next lecture, and may retry missed questions once.

FINAL ORAL EXAM (5 questions, 60% of final grade)
Score each answer 0 to 4:
4 = Correct concepts, applied precisely to the scenario, conclusion justified, no prompting needed.
3 = Sound, with minor gaps, or needed one follow-up.
2 = Correct concepts but weak application or unjustified conclusion.
1 = Uses the terms without showing understanding.
0 = Wrong, or no substantive answer.
The maximum is 20 points, and the student needs 14 to pass.

FINAL RESULT
Pass: at least 70% on the lecture questions (42 of 60) and at least 14 of 20 on the final exam. Both are required.
Pass with distinction: at least 54 of 60 on the lecture questions and at least 18 of 20 on the final exam.
Fail: below either threshold.
Final grade = (lecture-question % x 0.4) + (final-exam % x 0.6).
Retake: one retake of the final exam, at least 48 hours later, with new scenarios.
